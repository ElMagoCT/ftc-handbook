Season & Strategy

Read the game before you build the robot.

The teams that win are not the ones with the fanciest mechanism. They are the ones who figured out, in the first week, exactly which points to go after and built the simplest robot that gets them. This page is that first week, plus everything that follows it.

Kickoff weekend: game analysis

Do this before anyone opens Onshape. It takes one long afternoon and it saves a month.

  1. Read the manual. The whole thing. Split the sections across the team, then everyone reads the game rules and robot rules themselves. Write down every number: point values, time limits, expansion limits, protected zones.
  2. List every way to score. One row per scoring action. Points in auto, points in TeleOp, points in endgame. Note which ones need a human player and which need the alliance partner.
  3. Estimate a cycle time for each. Walk the field drawing with a tape measure. A robot drives about 3 to 5 feet per second. Add pickup and place time. Be honest, then add 30 percent.
  4. Compute points per second. Points divided by cycle time. Sort the table. The top two rows are your robot. Everything else is a bonus for later.
  5. Check the constraints. Can a robot reach the high goal inside the expansion limit? How many game elements fit in the 18-inch cube at start? Is defense legal near the scoring zone?
  6. Pick a strategy, then an architecture. "Cycle from the loading zone to the low goal, park in endgame" is a strategy. "Mecanum, two-stage slides, roller intake" is an architecture. Strategy first, always.
  7. Write it in the notebook. The table, the reasoning, the rejected options. This becomes portfolio page 3 and the Think Award interview answer in one shot.

Questions that decide the robot

  • What is the single highest points-per-second action a simple robot can do?
  • Where do game elements come from? Floor, human player, a dispenser? That decides the intake.
  • How high is the highest goal worth building for? That decides lift type and expansion.
  • What does a great auto score, and what sensors does it need?
  • What does the endgame pay, and how much of TeleOp does it cost?
  • Is there a task most teams will skip? That is where a mid-level team steals points.
Tip

Watch last year's Worlds finals for any game and count cycles. Top teams typically finish a cycle every 5 to 8 seconds. That number is the target, and it tells you how fast your slides and intake really need to be.

Cycle time is the whole game

Two minutes of TeleOp. Every second you cut from a cycle shows up directly in the score.

Where time goes

Driving

Usually half the cycle. Faster drivetrain gearing helps less than you think; a driver who does not overshoot helps more. Holonomic drives save the turn-and-line-up time.

Where time goes

Acquiring

The intake. A roller that grabs on contact is one second. A claw that has to be aimed is three. That difference is 20 elements a match.

Where time goes

Scoring

Lift travel and release. Cascade slides and a fast release (a servo flap or a roller kick-out) keep this under two seconds.

Ways to buy back seconds detail
  • Do two things at once. Raise the lift while driving to the goal. Retract while driving back. This is code, not hardware, and it is the biggest single gain.
  • Automate the repeated motion. One button for "go to scoring height," one for "return home." Drivers should never hold a stick and watch a lift.
  • Sensors instead of eyes. A color or distance sensor in the intake stops the rollers automatically and tells the driver they have the element. No more looking.
  • Field-centric driving on holonomic drivetrains so the driver never has to think about which way the robot faces.
  • Shorten the path. Sometimes the lower goal, closer to the source, wins on points per second even though it pays less per element.

Strategy archetypes

Most winning FTC robots are one of these. Pick one on purpose.

The cycler

Does one scoring task extremely fast and reliably, all match. Simple mechanisms, fast driver, tight code.

Good
  • Highest ceiling for a mid-budget team
  • Easy to practice and tune
  • Reliability is built in
Watch
  • Depends on a source of elements
  • Vulnerable to defense if legal

The specialist

Owns one high-value task most teams cannot do (a hard endgame, a high goal, a tricky auto). Pairs well with a cycler partner.

Good
  • Gets picked in alliance selection
  • Strong Innovate story
Watch
  • All eggs in one mechanism
  • Hard to rank high in quals alone

The auto-heavy robot

Scores most of its points in the first 30 seconds with localization and a fast autonomous, then plays support.

Good
  • Control Award material
  • Consistent points before any driver mistakes
Watch
  • Partner collisions ruin autos
  • Needs a coder with time

The everything robot

Tries to do every task. Almost never finished in time. Only viable for veteran teams with a full pre-season of testing.

Good
  • Flexible in playoffs
Watch
  • Everything is 80 percent done at the first event
  • Heavy, slow, complex to maintain
  • Two mechanisms fighting for the same space

From strategy to a robot on the field

A schedule that works

  • Week 1
    Game analysis and strategy. Cardboard mockups of the two candidate mechanisms. Order long-lead parts (slides, motors, servos).
  • Week 2–3
    Drivetrain driving. Reuse last year's if it is sound. Code gets a real robot to test on. CAD the first mechanism.
  • Week 4–5
    Mechanism v1 on the robot. It will be ugly. Test it against the real field element for hours. Start the auto.
  • Week 6
    First event. Whatever works, works. Film every match. Portfolio draft one.
  • After
    Iterate. Fix the top two point-losers between events. Never redesign the whole robot mid-season.

Decisions to make on purpose

Drivetrain
Mecanum unless you have a specific reason. See Drivetrains.
Build system
goBILDA or REV, not both on the same robot. See Build systems.
Motor budget
8 motors, 8 servos total. Four go to the drive. Plan the other four before you design.
Sensors
Odometry for auto, a limit switch on every slide, a sensor in the intake. Decide now so the mounts exist.
Software
Path-following library and code structure chosen in week 1 so hardware names and units match. See Code.
Who decides
One student lead per subsystem. Design reviews weekly, with the builders and coders in the room.

Scouting

You cannot pick a good alliance partner from memory. Write things down.

What to record per team

  • Auto: what it scores, from which start position, how consistent (out of the matches you watched).
  • TeleOp: which goals they score in, rough cycles per match, whether they need the human player.
  • Endgame: can they do it, how long does it take them.
  • Reliability: disconnects, mechanisms jamming, dead time.
  • Drivers: calm or frantic. Do they get in their partner's way.
  • Pit notes: robot condition, spare parts, whether they can run a second auto path.

Making the pick list

  • Rank by what complements your robot, not by raw score. If you score high, you want a partner with a great auto and a clean endgame.
  • Use FTCScout for OPR and match history, then correct it with what you saw. Stats miss the robot that broke down twice.
  • Have the list on paper before alliance selection. Names, numbers, first and second choice, and who to say no to.
  • If you are a likely pick, know your answer. Declining a captain is legal but has consequences for ranking points and reputation.

Drive team

Role

Driver

Moves the robot. Needs the most practice hours by far. Same person all season if possible.

Role

Operator

Runs the mechanisms on the second gamepad. Calls out state ("have it", "up", "clear").

Role

Human player

Game-specific. Feeds elements or interacts with the field. Practice this as much as driving.

Role

Coach

Watches the clock and the other alliance. The only person who talks strategy during the match. Can be a student.

Practice plan

  • Full 2:30 matches with a timer, on a full field, with a partner robot if you can borrow one.
  • Drills: 10 cycles as fast as possible, then 10 cycles with zero misses. Track both times week over week.
  • Practice the endgame with 40 seconds on the clock, not 30. Match nerves cost time.
  • Swap in the backup driver for at least one practice a week. People get sick on event day.
  • Every practice ends with a robot inspection: loose screws, wire strain relief, battery voltage.

Pre-match checklist

  • Fresh battery, voltage checked, strapped in.
  • Driver Hub charged, gamepads plugged in, correct OpMode selected, robot on the right alliance color.
  • Robot inside the sizing cube, preload set, mechanism in start position.
  • Auto start position marked on the tile and the robot lined up to it.
  • Talked to the alliance partner: whose auto goes where, who takes which goal, who does endgame first.
  • Robot sign visible, team shirts on, one person in the pit answering judges.

Event day

What to bring

  • Two printed portfolios. One for the Initial Interview, one for the pit table.
  • The engineering notebook, even though it is no longer judged. Judges still ask about your process, and it is where the answers live.
  • Spare motors, servos, a spare Control Hub if you own one, every cable type, zip ties, hex drivers, a multimeter.
  • Laptop with the code, a USB-C cable that works, and the last known-good build committed to Git.
  • Batteries: at least four, plus the charger. Label them and rotate.
  • Field tiles or at least the game elements for a pit practice area if the venue allows it.

Order of operations

  1. Check in, get the schedule. Inspection first, then judging, then matches. Know your interview time before you unpack.
  2. Inspection. Robot rules checklist. Have the servo spec sheet and the wiring visible. Fix and re-inspect fast; inspectors want you to pass.
  3. Initial Interview. Whole team, robot if you can bring it, portfolio in hand. See Judging.
  4. Matches. Someone always scouting, someone always in the pit.
  5. Alliance selection. Pick list ready. Coach speaks.
  6. Debrief on the bus. Three things that lost points, one thing to keep. Write it down before you forget.
Rule

Judges are only allowed to use what they learn at this event, this season. Match results, rankings, and social media do not count for judged awards. Your interview and portfolio are the whole picture. More on judging.

Missions

Mission S1

Break down a past game

  • Pick a game from Past Games and read its manual's scoring section.
  • Build the scoring table above with real numbers.
  • Write one paragraph on what you would build and why.
Done when a teammate can read your table and name your top two scoring actions without asking.
Mission S2

Scout a livestream

  • Watch six qualification matches from any event on the FTC YouTube channel or a regional stream.
  • Fill out the scouting sheet for every robot.
  • Make a pick list for the top-ranked team and defend it.
Done when your list has a first pick, a second pick, and a reason for each that is not "they scored a lot."
Mission S3

Cycle-time audit

  • Film your robot (or last year's) doing ten cycles.
  • Time each phase: drive, acquire, score, return.
  • Find the one phase to shorten and propose a specific fix.
Done when you have a number per phase and a change that would save at least one second.
Mission S4

Run a practice match day

  • Set up a full field, a timer, and a scorer.
  • Run five matches with the full drive team, including pre-match checklist and alliance-partner talk.
  • Score them honestly and debrief after each.
Done when match five scores higher than match one and you know why.
