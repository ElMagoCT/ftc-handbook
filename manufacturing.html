Materials & Manufacturing

Pick the material for the job, then make the part fit the first time.

What each material is good for, how each process works, and the numbers (temperatures, tolerances, thicknesses) that took us a few seasons to get right. Nothing here needs a machine shop, but it tells you what to ask for if you have access to one.

Alternatives

Studica, TETRIX, AndyMark

  • Studica: 3 mm anodized aluminum with a pattern that allows 16 mounting angles, M3 fasteners, Maverick motors. Comes in colors. Holes are not goBILDA-sized, so parts need drilling to mix.
  • TETRIX: the classic kit-of-parts. Simple, heavy, limited.
  • AndyMark: NeveRest motors, the official StarterBot kits, and raw stock.
  • Amazon / AliExpress: odd brackets, aluminum extrusion, silicone tubing, bearings by the bag. Check dimensions twice.
Shafts, bearings, and hubs detail
  • Hex shafts key into hubs with no set screws. 5 mm hex is the motor standard; goBILDA's 8 mm REX carries drivetrain loads.
  • D-shafts (round with a flat) rely on set screws and slip under torque. Use them only where the vendor gives you no choice.
  • Flanged ball bearings on every shaft that carries load, ideally two per shaft spaced apart. A shaft supported at one end is a lever that bends its bearing.
  • Shaft collars hold axial position, not torque. Two collars per shaft, snug against the bearings.
  • Dead axle vs live axle: a dead axle is fixed and the wheel spins on bearings around it (stronger, simpler). A live axle spins with the wheel (needed when you drive it from the end).

Tip

Aluminum for anything that carries load. Polycarbonate for anything that guides a game element. Printed plastic for anything with a complicated shape that does not carry much load. That rule of thumb covers 90 percent of an FTC robot.

Temperatures are starting points; use the spool's range. Shore hardness for TPU: 95A is firm, 85A is grippy, 60A is squishy. Lower is softer.

Settings that matter

  • Walls beat infill. 3 to 4 perimeters and 30–35% infill is the default for a structural part. Add walls before adding infill.
  • Layer height 0.2 mm for most parts. 0.28 mm for big brackets you do not care about.
  • Orientation decides strength. Parts are 30–50% weaker across layers. Orient so the load runs along the layers, and print gears flat.
  • Thin sections under 0.15 in (4 mm) snap. Add a fillet or a rib instead of hoping.
  • Overhangs past 45° need support. Design them out with chamfers when you can.
  • Holes print small. Design them 0.2 mm oversize or drill to size after.

Tolerancing printed parts

These are the numbers we use. Total clearance, applied to the CAD dimension of the printed part.

FitTotal clearanceUse
Loose0.02 in (0.5 mm)Screws through holes, parts that slide, quick assembly
Tight0.01 in (0.25 mm)Shafts that spin without wobble, pins, snug lids
Very tight (press)0.005 in (0.13 mm)Bearings, gears on hex shafts, anything that must not move
Note

For enclosed shapes (a round hole, a hex pocket, a square slot) split the clearance across both sides: a 0.01 in tight fit means 0.005 in per side, because every wall gets toleranced and the removal doubles.

Heat-set inserts, self-tapping bosses, and printed threads detail
  • Heat-set inserts (brass, M3 or M4) give printed parts real threads. Boss hole = insert outer diameter + about 0.3 mm. Press in with a soldering iron at 200 °C, slowly.
  • Self-tapping into plastic: core hole at about 90% of the screw's major diameter (2.7 mm for M3). Fine for parts you will not disassemble often.
  • Printed threads work above M8 with a thread FeatureScript. Below that, use an insert.
  • Through-holes with a nut on the back are still the most reliable. Design a hex pocket for the nut so it cannot spin.
Resin (SLA) printing detail
  • Very fine detail and smooth surfaces. Standard resin is brittle; tough or engineering resin is usable for small gears and precise jigs.
  • Nylon-like resins were the most durable material we found for differential swerve gears, ahead of every FDM filament we tried.
  • Wash in isopropyl alcohol, cure under UV, wear gloves. Resin is a skin irritant, and uncured resin is not allowed near a competition field.
  • Expensive per part and slow to post-process. Use it for the two parts that need it, not the whole robot.

Sheet metal and water jet

Water jet

  • High-pressure water with garnet abrasive cuts aluminum plate with no heat and no burr worth mentioning.
  • Design with no tolerancing. The water jet software compensates for the kerf. Draw the part at nominal size.
  • We use 1/16, 1/8, 3/16, and 1/4 in aluminum. 1/8 in is the workhorse for plates and brackets.
  • Part must fit the bed; on ours the cut area is under 12 × 12 in. Check the machine you are using.
  • Export a DXF of the flat face from Onshape. Include the holes; drilling later is where the misalignment comes from.

Bending and finishing

  • Use Onshape's sheet metal mode so the flat pattern accounts for bend allowance (the K-factor). A hand-drawn flat will be the wrong length.
  • Bend on a brake or in a vise with a hardwood block. Bend radius about equal to the thickness. Minimum flange about four times thickness.
  • Tap the sheet instead of adding nuts: M4 in 1/8 in aluminum gives about 3 mm of thread, which is enough for a bracket. Use a tap guide and cutting fluid.
  • Deburr every edge with a file or deburring tool. Inspectors check for sharp edges, and so does your hand.
Lightening patterns detail
  • Solid plate is heavy. Cut triangles or hexagons out of areas that are not on a load path.
  • Keep a minimum web (the material between holes) of about 0.1 in in 1/8 in aluminum. Thinner webs flex and tear on the water jet.
  • Do not lighten around bearing bores or motor mounts. Leave a solid ring at least one hole-diameter wide.
  • Onshape: sketch pattern of triangles, one extrude-remove. Drive the count with a variable so you can dial it back if the part flexes.

Other processes

Hand tools

  • Hacksaw or miter saw with a non-ferrous blade for channel and extrusion. Deburr after.
  • Drill press with a center punch beats a hand drill every time for accuracy.
  • Step drill for polycarbonate; regular bits grab and crack it.
  • Score-and-snap polycarbonate up to about 1/8 in with a utility knife and a straightedge.
  • Metal ruler, calipers, and a marker. Measure twice; aluminum is not free.

Laser cutting

  • Polycarbonate, acrylic, plywood, and cardboard. Not PVC, ever. Not metal unless it is a fiber laser.
  • Kerf 0.1–0.3 mm. Let the cutter software offset, or design tab-and-slot joints with thickness plus 0.1 mm.
  • Mask the material to avoid burn marks. Test a small cut first.
  • Great for intake side plates, sponsor panels, and quick prototype brackets in 1/4 in plywood.

CNC router or mill

  • Fusion 360 or Onshape CAM generates the toolpath from your CAD. A sponsor's shop can run it if you have a STEP and a drawing.
  • Inside corners get a radius at least equal to the end mill. Design 1/8 in fillets and you can use a 1/4 in tool.
  • Tolerance ±0.005 in is normal for aluminum. Press-fit bearings want a proper bore, not a drilled hole.
  • 6061 for nearly everything. 7075 for hubs and high-stress parts.

Fastening and assembly

  • Screws with nylon-insert lock nuts for anything you might remove. Rivets for polycarbonate panels you will not.
  • Blue thread locker on set screws and anything that vibrates. Never red.
  • Pilot holes: 3.3 mm for an M4 tap, 4.3–4.5 mm for M4 clearance.
  • Zip ties and adhesive are for wires and quick fixes, not structure.
  • Document the process in the notebook with photos. Judges ask how you made it.

Prototyping ladder

Cheap and fast first. Commit to aluminum only after the idea works.

  1. Cardboard and a drill. Prove the geometry and the grab. One afternoon.
  2. Plywood or PLA on kit parts. Prove the mechanism on the actual robot with a real motor. Test 100 cycles.
  3. PETG and polycarbonate. Competition version one. Fix what broke in step two.
  4. Aluminum where it failed. Replace only the parts that bent, wore, or cracked. Keep the rest printed.

Rules that touch manufacturing

R101
The team builds the robot and its major mechanisms. Sponsor machining is fine; sponsor designing and building a mechanism is not.
R302
Raw materials and COTS parts can be cut, drilled, painted, and modified.
R301
No COTS mechanism purpose-built for a game task, except COTS drive chassis and official StarterBots.
R305
No replicas of game elements built into the robot.
R202
No hazardous materials. That includes uncured resin and anything that flakes or leaks onto the field.
Sharp edges
Inspectors check. Deburr, fillet printed parts, cap zip ties flush.
3D printing
No specific rule. Printed parts are custom parts like any other.
Cost
No cost limit, but a first season runs roughly $2,000–3,000 including registration. Budget for spares.

Missions

Mission M1

Tolerance test coupon

  • Print a plate with 5 mm hex pockets at 0.005, 0.01, and 0.02 in total clearance.
  • Test-fit a real hex shaft in each.
  • Record which fit is which for your printer and filament.
Done when the team has a printed reference card taped to the printer.
Mission M2

Bracket, three ways

  • Design one motor bracket. Make it in PETG, in polycarbonate by hand, and in 1/8 in aluminum (water jet or SendCutSend).
  • Weigh each and load-test each until it fails or deforms.
Done when you have a table of weight, cost, time, and failure load for all three.
Mission M3

Sheet metal tray

  • Model a battery tray in Onshape sheet metal mode.
  • Export the flat pattern, cut it, bend it, tap two holes for mounting.
Done when the battery fits and the tray bolts to the chassis with no extra nuts.
Mission M4

Printed gear wear test

  • Print the same gear in PLA, PETG, and nylon (or resin).
  • Run each against a metal gear under load for one hour on a test rig.
  • Photograph and measure the wear.
Done when you can recommend a material for printed gears with a photo to back it up.
